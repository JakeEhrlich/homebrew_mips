Grit Block Diagram

Microcoded: every flash word is a microinstruction and there is no state machine. 11 GALs, 19 chips. A microop takes two clocks, fetch then execute, so the two shared buses are free in the execute clock. Blue is the data bus, gold the address bus, green point-to-point. Each block lists the lines it takes; all of them come from the microinstruction register.

The microword

BitsFieldMeaning in the execute clock
15:14DRVwho drives D: 00 nobody, 01 memory at A, 10 the ALU, 11 the next flash word (a literal; the PC drives the address and steps past it)
13:12LDwho loads D at the end of the clock: 00 nobody, 01 A, 10 B, 11 PC
11WRmemory at A takes D; the pulse is the first half of the clock
10:9FALU function: 00 add, 01 and, 10 nor, 11 pass B. When LD is PC and DRV is a literal, bit 9 set means "only if A == B"
8, 7AIDXat the fetch edge, before this word executes, A becomes 0x8000 + 2 x idx: the address of register idx (bit 8 for the high byte, bit 7 for the low; the assembler sets both)
5:1idxregister number, 0 to 31
6, 0spare

The two clocks of a microop

fetch    PC drives A; flash drives D; MIR loads D[15:9]; if AIDX, A loads 0x8000 | idx<<1; PC += 2
execute  A drives the address (or the PC, for a literal); the DRV choice drives D;
         the LD choice loads at the end; WR pulses WE# in the first half;
         a literal also steps the PC past the literal word

Immediates

There is no immediate field wide enough to be a value, so an immediate is a literal: a microop with DRV = 11 is followed by a data word. In that microop's execute clock the PC, which has already stepped to the data word, drives the address bus, the flash drives D with it, and whatever LD names loads it. The PC then steps past the data word as well. So B = 0x1234 is two words and two clocks:

DRV=lit LD=B        ; execute: flash[PC] -> D -> B
0x1234              ; the literal, skipped by the PC

A jump is the same thing aimed at the PC: DRV=lit LD=PC then the target address, with bit 9 set for "only if A == B". An addiu is a load of the register into A, a literal into B, the add parked into B, and a store to the destination register: five words including the literal, eight clocks. The 5-bit idx field is the one small immediate that rides inside the word, and it only ever means "the address of register idx".

The word after a literal-taking microop is data, so the assembler owns that layout; a program written in raw microops just has to remember it. Registers are SRAM words at 0x8000 + 2n; the idx field is what keeps them one word away instead of three.
